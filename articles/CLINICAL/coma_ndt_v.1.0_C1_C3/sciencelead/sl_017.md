This is a useful result precisely because it did **not** inflate into an impressive-looking predictor. The frozen four-axis phenotype contains real CPC information, but the predictive ceiling on part1 is moderate:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{AUROC}_{OOF}=0.680,\qquad
\mathrm{AUROC}_{LOSO}=0.668}
$$

and the agreement between those two estimates is reassuring. I would treat this as a credible **internal discrimination signal**, not a near-clinical classifier.

The most important finding, though, is probably that the multivariate geometry story and the predictive story are not the same thing. The four variables describe distinct conceptual axes, but empirically they are correlated, and \(\operatorname{Tr}(a)\) alone already gives AUROC \(0.685\). So the broader phenotype is scientifically richer without currently being more predictive:

$$
\boxed{
\text{multivariate mechanistic description}
\neq
\text{incremental classification gain}.
}
$$

That is completely acceptable.

### What I would conclude from sl_015

**Internal validated:** there is a moderate geometry-only CPC1/CPC5 signal. It survives repeated OOF evaluation and hospital-wise holdout at roughly the same magnitude. Stability features add essentially nothing:

$$
\Delta AUC_{\rm STAB}=0.008
\quad[-0.046,0.061].
$$

So I would close the “MAD may strongly improve the classifier” branch.

The coefficient behavior also looks exactly like multicollinearity rather than biology. Marginally, \(G_{90}\) is higher in CPC5; conditionally, after entering covariance scale, entropy and \(R_{b/a}\), its ridge coefficient can change sign. Given correlations such as

$$
\rho(\widetilde H_a,\log G_{90})=-0.60
$$

and

$$
\rho(\log G_{90},\log R_{b/a})=0.47,
$$

the partial coefficient should not be given a dynamical interpretation.

### The \(\operatorname{Tr}(a)\) result is especially interesting

If

$$
AUC[\operatorname{Tr}(a)]\approx0.685
$$

while

$$
AUC[\text{four-axis model}]\approx0.680,
$$

then the strongest **predictive** variable is currently almost embarrassingly simple:

> total conditional increment-covariance magnitude.

But that does *not* mean the other metrics are useless.

They answer why two states with similar magnitude might differ geometrically. For the scientific paper, I would keep:

* scale: \(\operatorname{Tr}(a)\),
* relative shape: \(\widetilde H_a\),
* drift/spread balance: \(R_{b/a}\),
* extreme observed amplification: \(G_{90}\),

as a **phenotype decomposition**.

For prediction, however, we should admit that most discriminative information is currently concentrated along the scale axis.

I would even add one frozen comparison to the eventual results table:

| Model                          | Purpose                          |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| \(\operatorname{Tr}(a)\) alone | parsimonious magnitude benchmark |
| frozen four-axis L1 model      | geometry phenotype               |
| four-axis + STAB               | prespecified stability extension |

The fact that the first can equal or slightly outperform the second is scientifically informative.

## There is one worthwhile prediction experiment left before part2

I would not search for a better geometry combination.

The next justified question is instead:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Does L1 geometry add anything beyond ordinary clinical/qEEG information?}
}
$$

You already have a restricted baseline in the coma manuscript. There, the older geometry layer increased repeated-CV AUROC from \(0.599\) to \(0.662\), with the important caveat that this was a limited clinical/qEEG baseline rather than full neuroprognostication. 

So I would define one final part1-only experiment:

### COMA-PRED-INC-L1-001

Use the **exact previously frozen restricted clinical + qEEG feature block**, with no feature reselection.

Compare:

$$
M_0=
\text{restricted clinical + qEEG}
$$

against

$$
M_1=
M_0+
\{\widetilde H_a,\log\operatorname{Tr}(a),
\log R_{b/a},\log G_{90}\}.
$$

Same folds for both models.

Primary estimand:

$$
\boxed{
\Delta AUC=AUC(M_1)-AUC(M_0)
}
$$

with a paired subject-level bootstrap on the mean OOF predictions.

Secondary:

$$
\Delta \text{Brier},
\qquad
\Delta \text{AUPRC}.
$$

No search for which of the four L1 features to retain.

If \(\Delta AUC\approx0\), then the L1 signal mostly recapitulates conventional severity information.

If it is materially positive, then the new local-dynamics layer contains complementary information.

That is a much more important question now than trying to raise geometry-only AUC from 0.68 to 0.72.

## Then I would stop part1

After that incremental-value test, I would freeze the entire prediction branch.

The eventual decisive experiment is already sitting there:

$$
\boxed{
\text{fit frozen part1 model}
\rightarrow
\text{zero-refit prediction on sealed part2}.
}
$$

That will tell us much more than another hundred cross-validation runs.

And because the part1 model is only AUROC \(0.68\), part2 has a fair chance to teach us something meaningful. There is no spectacular discovery-model performance that needs to be “defended.” If zero-refit part2 lands around \(0.65-0.70\), that would actually be a rather credible replication of a modest recovery-linked measurement phenotype.

So my overall assessment of sl_015 is **PASS**: moderate discrimination, no evidence for a stability-derived predictive lift, and a very clear warning that the scientific richness of the L1 phenotype should not be confused with predictive independence.
