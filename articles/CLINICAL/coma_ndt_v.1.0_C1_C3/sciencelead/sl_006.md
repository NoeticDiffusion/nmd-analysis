This is now a very clean fork. I would **not unlock part2 on the stored subject-anchored families**. I would resolve Gate 1 prospectively by moving the confirmatory computation to the **cohort-anchored chart, with re-kNN performed in that same chart**.

The reason is methodological, not because the cohort-frame \(p\)-values are weaker. The H5 contract itself assigns subject-anchored coordinates to within-subject geometry and cohort-anchored coordinates to clinical group comparison.  More importantly, both frozen endpoints are metric-dependent. For

$$
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(a)
=
\frac{(\mathrm{tr}\,a)^2}{\mathrm{tr}(a^2)},
$$

an orthogonal transform or common scalar leaves the quantity unchanged, but a subject-specific anisotropic rescaling generally does not. And

$$
G_{90}
=
Q_{.90}
\frac{\|x_{j+1}-x_{i+1}\|+\varepsilon}
{\|x_j-x_i\|+\varepsilon}
$$

is likewise not invariant to arbitrary anisotropic axis scaling. The attenuation you found is therefore scientifically meaningful: **part of the discovery contrast depends on the subject-specific metric frame.**

### Why I prefer cohort re-kNN over cohort same-pair

If the clinical metric frame is changed, I think locality should change with it. `cohort_samepair` answers a useful diagnostic question—“what happens if we retain subject-frame neighbors but measure their distances in the common frame?”—but the proper cohort-frame estimand is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{select neighbors in cohort frame}
\rightarrow
\text{compute increments/distances in cohort frame}
}
$$

So I would freeze `cohort_reknn`, not `cohort_samepair`, for confirmation.

That gives the part1 prospective expectation:

$$
d_{\mathrm{eff}}:\ d\approx0.27
$$

and

$$
G_{90}:\ d\approx-0.40,
$$

rather than the larger subject-frame effects. That is a more demanding confirmatory test, but if it replicates it is substantially harder to dismiss as a normalization artifact.

I would not claim that it *explains* the \(d_{\mathrm{eff}}\) attenuation, but it makes the frame question nontrivial rather than cosmetic.

## Where I think the science stands

The most interesting discovery is now actually more nuanced than the original one:

$$
\boxed{
\text{the recovery-linked L1 phenotype exists strongly in a subject-relative dynamical geometry,}
}
$$

but

$$
\boxed{
\text{its magnitude is not invariant to the common clinical metric frame.}
}
$$

That raises a useful scientific question for later work: does recovery relate partly to **within-person organization of dynamical degrees of freedom**, rather than simply to an absolute cross-person geometry? That's only a plausible interpretation today, but it may become important.

For the present paper, though, I would resist that theoretical branch until part2.
