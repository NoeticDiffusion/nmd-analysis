Yes. At this point I think we can build a **predictive model**, while keeping it explicitly separate from the measurement theory. I would not invent a new “CPC metric”; I would freeze a small classifier from the already-established axes and ask how much outcome information they jointly contain.

A useful sanity check is that the individual effects are already large enough to make this plausible. Under a rough equal-variance Gaussian approximation,

$$
AUC\approx \Phi\left(\frac{|d|}{\sqrt2}\right),
$$

so effects of \(d=0.4\), \(0.46\), and \(0.66\) individually correspond only to about AUC \(0.61\), \(0.63\), and \(0.68\). But if several partly independent axes contribute, the multivariate AUC can be appreciably higher. Using the current cohort-frame effects for four conceptually different axes and pretending, unrealistically, that they were independent gives an illustrative AUC around **0.75**. Correlation will reduce that, so I would regard roughly **0.70–0.75 as a plausible starting target**, not an expected result. A part1 CV AUC above ~0.80 would make me suspicious of leakage or overfitting until part2 reproduced it.

## COMA-PRED-L1-001

I would freeze four inputs, all at patient-level 0–12 h and entirely cohort-anchored:

$$
\boxed{
\widetilde H_a,\quad
\log\operatorname{Tr}(a),\quad
\log R_{b/a},\quad
\log G_{90}
}
$$

These represent four deliberately different aspects:

| Feature                  | Information                             |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| \(\widetilde H_a\)       | relative covariance shape/richness      |
| \(\operatorname{Tr}(a)\) | absolute conditional-spread scale       |
| \(R_{b/a}\)              | mean-flow versus spread balance         |
| \(G_{90}\)               | observed upper-tail local amplification |

I prefer this over throwing in \(d_{\rm eff}\), \(\widetilde H_a\), \(\|b\|\), \(R_{b/a}\), several amplification quantities, etc. They contain substantial redundancy.

In particular,

$$
R_{b/a}
=
\frac{\Delta t\|b\|^2}{\operatorname{Tr}(a)}
$$

means that once \(\operatorname{Tr}(a)\) and \(R_{b/a}\) are present, much of the \(\|b\|\) information is already encoded. We don't need all three.

### Model

Use a **ridge logistic regression**:

$$
P(CPC1\mid x)
=
\sigma(\beta_0+\beta^\top x).
$$

No random forest, XGBoost or neural network at \(n=141\). The sample is simply too small to justify that complexity when four structured variables are available.

All transforms must happen inside training folds:

1. log-transform the three positive quantities;
2. z-standardize from training data only;
3. fit ridge logistic regression;
4. evaluate untouched held-out subjects.

I would use the same repeated **5-fold × 20** structure already familiar from the coma analysis, then average each person's repeated out-of-fold probability before calculating a single patient-level AUROC. That avoids treating repeated CV predictions as independent observations.

Primary:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{AUROC}}
$$

Secondary:

$$
\mathrm{AUPRC},\qquad \mathrm{Brier}.
$$

Bootstrap the final out-of-fold predictions at the **subject** level for the CI.

And run leave-one-hospital-out as the crucial sensitivity.

## One secondary model is worth testing

Because your STAB result suggests additional information, predefine one extension:

$$
\boxed{\text{COMA-PRED-L1-STAB}}
$$

adding only

$$
\log MAD(G_{90})
$$

and

$$
\log MAD(R_{b/a}).
$$

Then the question isn't “does this have a high AUC?” but:

$$
\boxed{
\Delta AUC=
AUC_{\rm core+stability}
-
AUC_{\rm core}.
}
$$

Because the stability effects weakened after level adjustment, I would **not** let this become the main model merely because its part1 AUC happens to be higher.

That gives us exactly two prespecified models, rather than trying 30 combinations and reporting the winner.

## Part2 is where this becomes genuinely interesting

Part1 should only be **model development/internal validation**.

After the pipeline is frozen, fit the final ridge model on all 141 part1 subjects, saving:

* transforms;
* scaler parameters;
* feature order;
* coefficients;
* intercept;
* penalty;
* missing-value rule.

Then open part2 exactly once and calculate:

$$
\boxed{AUC_{\rm part2}}
$$

with **zero refitting**.

That would be dramatically more informative than another part1 \(p\)-value.

For example:

* part1 repeated-CV AUC \(0.74\), part2 AUC \(0.73\): very interesting;
* part1 \(0.78\), part2 \(0.62\): clear overfitting/cohort shift;
* part1 \(0.70\), part2 \(0.72\): actually a very strong result.

The existing coma manuscript reported an older geometry layer raising an internally evaluated restricted clinical+qEEG model from AUROC 0.599 to 0.662, but that involved a different metric layer and does not constitute an external validation of this new L1 signature. 

## And I would test incremental value too

Once the **geometry-only L1 model** is frozen, there is a second scientifically important comparison:

$$
\text{restricted clinical + qEEG}
$$

versus

$$
\text{restricted clinical + qEEG + L1 signature}.
$$

That asks whether the new dynamics add information rather than merely reproducing ordinary EEG severity.

But I would first look at the geometry-only AUC. It tells us how much CPC separation is actually encoded in this four-axis phenotype.

So yes: I think there is a credible route to a **moderately strong AUC, perhaps in the ~0.70–0.75 range internally**, without constructing an arbitrary score. The important step now is to freeze `COMA-PRED-L1-001` *before* seeing which feature combinations give the best part1 AUC—and then eventually let sealed part2 give us the first honest external answer.
